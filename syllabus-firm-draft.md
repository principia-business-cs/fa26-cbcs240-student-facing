# CBCS240 Technology and Innovation - Student Syllabus

## Course Description

CBCS240 Technology and Innovation is an experiential course about how people recognize opportunities, use technology as leverage, test ideas with real people, and make disciplined decisions about risk, value, and future impact.

This course is not only about startups or product ideas. You will explore several innovation paths, including investing, building a local business, creating a product, launching a platform, improving an organization, and working with emerging technologies.

## Core Course Question

How do people create, evaluate, adopt, and scale new value through technology, and what tradeoffs do those paths create in real life?

## Course Loop

We will use this loop throughout the semester:

> Goal -> Data -> Experiment -> Sensemaking -> Adjustment

Innovation is not a perfect linear plan. You will practice learning, testing, adapting, and communicating your reasoning.

## Textbook and Readings

The course will draw from:

Joe Tidd and John R. Bessant, *Managing Innovation: Integrating Technological, Market and Organizational Change*, 8th edition. Wiley.

No individual textbook purchase is required unless announced separately. Required readings will be linked in Canvas or provided through official course resources.

## Innovation Tracks

You will explore innovation through these tracks:

- Investor / evaluator
- Local business builder
- Long-term wealth builder
- Platform founder
- Product creator
- Technology innovator

These tracks are not permanent labels. They are lenses for thinking about opportunity, risk, money, stress, family life, community impact, and future work.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, you should be able to:

1. Explain major concepts in innovation management.
2. Identify opportunities created by unmet needs, field changes, technology shifts, and community problems.
3. Learn from people through observation, interviews, feedback, and field research.
4. Map a field by identifying customers, constraints, competitors, alternatives, technologies, and failure modes.
5. Frame and reframe problems based on evidence.
6. Generate multiple possible responses before committing to one solution.
7. Build low-fidelity prototypes or test artifacts.
8. Use feedback and evidence to revise, pivot, narrow, broaden, or stop.
9. Evaluate opportunities, threats, risks, and opportunity costs.
10. Explain the business logic of an innovation.
11. Analyze the personal and social tradeoffs of different innovation paths.
12. Present an innovation case clearly through writing, visuals, and speech.

## Regular Class Rhythm

Most class meetings will include:

1. Opening question or short case
2. Concept anchor
3. Applied activity
4. Pair or team sensemaking
5. Exit ticket or next-step commitment

## AI and Technology

You may use AI and digital tools for brainstorming, research support, drafting, prototyping, and analysis when allowed by the assignment. You are responsible for verifying claims, citing sources where required, and explaining your own reasoning.

AI may not replace evidence from real people, field observation, or your own sensemaking.

## Collaboration

Some work will be individual and some will be team-based. Team assignments may include individual reflections or contribution checks.

## Participation

Because this is an experiential course, participation matters. We will use class time for interviews, prototypes, peer feedback, and studio work. Missing class means missing part of the learning process.

## Final Note

The goal is not to guess perfectly or produce a flawless idea. The goal is to become better at noticing, learning, testing, adapting, and communicating under uncertainty.
