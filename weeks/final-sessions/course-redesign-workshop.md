# Redesign This Course: An Innovation Workshop

CBCS240: Technology and Innovation | Session 1 of our final three classes | 50 minutes

No video preparation is required. Today, use your experience in this course as evidence for designing an improvement. Leave with a problem, a goal, a proposed change, and a way to test it.

Our learning loop: **Set a goal -> gather data -> experiment -> make sense -> adjust.** Return to an earlier step whenever new information changes your thinking.

## Our course objectives: Where did you experience them?

These objectives give us a way to reflect on the course. The activities below were opportunities to practice them; participating in an activity does not automatically mean an objective was achieved. Use your own experience to explain what helped and what could improve.

| Course objective | Examples from our course | A question for your reflection |
|---|---|---|
| Developing specific skills, competencies, and points of view needed by professionals in the field most closely related to this course | Defining a campus problem and goal; gathering information; learning and demonstrating a hard skill; considering risk, opportunity cost, and business value. | What can I now do or judge more effectively? What still needs practice? |
| Acquiring skills in working with others as a member of a team | Campus project groups, peer questions, sharing information, and responding with "I wonder..." and "Yes, and..." | How did I contribute, listen, handle disagreement, or help the group move forward? |
| Gaining a broader understanding and appreciation of intellectual/cultural activity (music, science, literature, etc.) | Exploring books, lectures, interviews, and podcasts about geography, wealth, government, economics, and retirement. | Which perspective expanded how I understand the world? |

You may have experienced these opportunities differently from your classmates. Honest disagreement and specific criticism are welcome.

## How the final three classes support your paper

1. **Today: Objectives and the innovation loop.** Gather evidence about your learning and propose a course improvement. Save examples for your course strengths, weaknesses, and personal learning.
2. **Next class: Books and big ideas.** Explore one resource or topic that interested you. Draft the part of your paper about how it affected your thinking.
3. **Last class: Five-minute innovation talks.** Explain your definition and learn from classmates. Draft or revise the part of your paper defining innovation.

These notes feed into one three-page final paper, not three additional essays.

## 1. Review the objectives and reflect individually | 0-8 minutes

Spend three minutes reviewing the objectives together, then five minutes writing privately:

1. When did you learn the most in this course? Describe a specific moment and what helped.
2. When were you confused, disengaged, or unsure what to do next? What did you need?
3. What can you now understand or do that you could not at the beginning?

Keep these notes for your [three-page final reflection](../../assignments/final-course-reflection.md). You may keep personal feedback private.

## 2. Interview a partner | 8-16 minutes

Take four minutes each as interviewer and interviewee. Listen before suggesting solutions.

- Can you describe a specific example?
- What were you trying to accomplish?
- What helped you, or got in your way?
- What would a better experience have made possible?

Record one observation and one question. Ask permission before sharing your partner's experience with others; do not attach their name.

## 3. Form a group and define the problem | 16-22 minutes

Join another pair. Compare observations and choose one learning need. Avoid assuming everyone had the same experience.

Complete:

> Students need a better way to __________ because our observations suggest __________.
>
> Our goal is to __________. We would know the change helped if __________.

What evidence supports your problem? What are you still assuming?

## 4. Generate and make an improvement tangible | 22-30 minutes

Generate three different ways to address your problem. Choose one and sketch it: a sample activity, revised assignment, project milestone, feedback process, or class schedule.

Include:

- What students and the instructor would actually do.
- How it would help the learning goal.
- One tradeoff: time, workload, cost, flexibility, or something else.
- One assumption that needs testing.

A proposal such as "make class more organized" needs a concrete example of what would change.

## 5. Try the prototype and gather feedback | 30-40 minutes

Pair with another group. Take five minutes per group to explain your learning problem and let the other group try one small part of your proposed activity, assignment instructions, or feedback process. Observe what they understand, where they hesitate, and what questions arise. If needed, three groups can rotate within the ten minutes.

Listeners contribute:

- **I wonder...** Ask about an assumption, an overlooked need, or a possible consequence.
- **Yes, and...** Build on the proposal with a specific addition.

Discuss the learning experience respectfully. Focus on activities and structures rather than judging individuals.

## 6. Revise and design a test | 40-46 minutes

Make sense of the trial: What did you observe, what do you think it means, and what remains uncertain? Use that evidence to revise your proposal. A short peer trial can reveal confusion, but it cannot establish a long-term learning improvement.

1. What is the smallest version we could try in one class?
2. What would we observe or collect to judge whether it helped?
3. What result would lead us to keep, change, or abandon it?

Describe a proposed test; do not claim the improvement works before testing it.

## 7. Individual exit ticket | 46-50 minutes

- How did this activity illustrate innovation?
- What changed in your thinking after listening to someone else?
- Which course objective did you practice today? What evidence supports your answer?
- Write three or four sentences you could develop in your paper: one strength, one weakness, and a specific improvement.

## Group output

Prepare one page or shared document containing your problem, goal, supporting observations, three possible improvements, selected proposal, tradeoff, and test. Be ready to share it with the instructor.

Your individual feedback may differ from your group's proposal. Constructive criticism is welcome; agreement with the instructor is not the goal.
